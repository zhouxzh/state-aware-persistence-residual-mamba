\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Forecasting Benchmarks and Long-Horizon Evaluation}

Forecasting science has long emphasized that model comparisons must be interpreted through the evaluation setting, the forecast horizon, and the diversity of temporal patterns present in the data.
Large empirical studies such as the M4 competition show that forecasting performance is highly dependent on the data-generating context and that no single modeling family dominates across all series and horizons \cite{m4}.
This observation is relevant to modern deep forecasting because long-horizon tasks combine several difficulties: multi-step error accumulation, changing signal-to-noise ratios across horizons, and sensitivity to distribution shift.
Recent surveys and forecasting studies therefore treat long-horizon prediction as a setting in which architecture design, normalization, horizon length, and evaluation protocol must be considered together \cite{tsf-survey,tft}.

The present manuscript follows this forecasting-science view by evaluating a fixed GLD task across horizons from 1 to 360 days rather than reporting a single horizon or a single aggregate score.
This choice is important because short-horizon and long-horizon performance can favor different models.
It also motivates the use of both a shared configuration and source-optimized comparisons: the shared protocol improves interpretability across model families, while source-optimized runs indicate how strong imported baselines behave when their own hyperparameters are respected.

\subsection{Long-Horizon Neural Forecasting Models}

Deep forecasting models have developed along several complementary directions.
N-BEATS introduced a neural basis-expansion architecture that remains an important reference point for interpretable and accurate univariate forecasting \cite{nbeats}.
N-HiTS extended this direction with hierarchical interpolation and multi-rate signal processing to improve long-horizon performance \cite{nhits}.
These models are not attention-based, but they are important because they show that explicit multi-scale structure can be competitive in long-horizon forecasting.

Transformer-based models introduced another major direction by using attention to model long temporal context.
Informer reduces the computational burden of attention for long sequence forecasting \cite{informer}, while Reformer provides a general efficient-attention design through locality-sensitive hashing and reversible layers \cite{reformer}.
Autoformer and FEDformer add decomposition mechanisms to the forecasting architecture, reflecting the practical value of separating slowly varying components from residual temporal variation \cite{autoformer,fedformer}.
Temporal Fusion Transformers address multi-horizon forecasting with interpretable attention and gating mechanisms, providing a journal-level reference for multi-horizon neural forecasting \cite{tft}.

More recent work has also questioned whether increasingly complex attention mechanisms are always necessary.
DLinear shows that simple decomposition-based linear models can be strong long-term forecasting baselines, especially when evaluation protocols are carefully controlled \cite{dlinear}.
PatchTST improves Transformer forecasting by segmenting time series into patches, reducing token length and encouraging local temporal representation learning \cite{patchtst}.
TimesNet reformulates temporal variation through two-dimensional representations and provides another strong general-purpose neural forecasting baseline \cite{timesnet}.
Together, these studies motivate the baseline set used in this manuscript: the comparison should include not only Mamba models, but also decomposition, patch-based, inverted, and multi-scale alternatives.

\subsection{Nonstationarity, Normalization, and Decomposition}

Nonstationarity is a central difficulty in long-horizon forecasting because the distribution observed during training may differ from the distribution encountered during testing.
Changes in level, scale, trend, volatility, or temporal regime can make a representation learned on past windows unreliable for future windows.
This issue is particularly important in financial forecasting, where empirical studies and reviews emphasize the impact of regime changes, noise, and unstable temporal dependence \cite{finance-dl-survey}.

Several forecasting methods address this problem by modifying either the input distribution or the model architecture.
RevIN normalizes each instance and then reverses the normalization at the output, directly targeting distribution shift between input windows and forecast targets \cite{revin}.
Non-stationary Transformers combine series stationarization with a de-stationary attention mechanism, arguing that forecasting models need both normalized inputs and a way to preserve useful nonstationary information \cite{nonstationary-transformer}.
Decomposition-based methods such as Autoformer, FEDformer, DLinear, and N-HiTS also reflect a related principle: separating trend, seasonal, or multi-scale components can make long-horizon prediction more stable than forcing all variation into a single latent representation \cite{autoformer,fedformer,dlinear,nhits}.

The NS-Mamba family studied here follows this line of work but applies it to a selective state-space backbone.
Rather than treating nonstationarity only as a preprocessing issue, the architecture combines reversible scaling, decomposition, and shift-aware fusion around the Mamba encoder.
This design is intended to expose level, scale, trend, and residual structure explicitly before those components are mixed by the sequence model.

\subsection{Inverted and Variable-Token Forecasting Architectures}

Inverted forecasting architectures reconsider the tokenization used by sequence models.
iTransformer treats variables as tokens and encodes each variable's temporal history as its representation, showing that this inverted view can be effective for multivariate forecasting \cite{itransformer}.
This design is relevant because many forecasting architectures inherit tokenization assumptions from natural language processing, where positions rather than variables are the primary tokens.
For time series, however, the choice of tokenization controls how temporal history, cross-variable dependence, and forecast heads interact.

The S\_Mamba backbone used in this study follows a related idea by embedding a variable's lookback history before applying Mamba encoder layers.
Although the GLD experiment is a single-target forecasting task, the inverted representation remains useful because it defines how the lookback window is compressed into hidden features before horizon projection.
This also makes S\_Mamba a natural starting point for studying whether explicit nonstationary modules can improve Mamba-based forecasting.

\subsection{Mamba-Based Time Series Forecasting}

Mamba provides linear-time sequence modeling through selective state spaces, making it attractive for long-context tasks where quadratic attention can be costly \cite{mamba}.
Recent studies have adapted this backbone to time series forecasting in different ways.
S\_Mamba evaluates whether Mamba is effective for forecasting and uses bidirectional Mamba layers to model inter-variate correlations with feed-forward layers for temporal dependencies \cite{smamba}.
Bi-Mamba+ extends bidirectional processing with a forget gate and relation-aware choices between channel-independent and channel-mixing tokenization \cite{bimamba}.
MambaTS analyzes limitations of directly applying Mamba to long-term forecasting and proposes temporal Mamba blocks, variable scanning strategies, and variable-aware scan ordering \cite{mambats}.
TimeMachine uses multiple Mamba pathways to handle channel-mixing and channel-independence settings while preserving linear scalability \cite{timemachine}.
TSMamba moves toward a Mamba-based foundation model for time series prediction using bidirectional encoders and transfer learning \cite{tsmamba}.

These studies show that Mamba has become an active forecasting backbone, but most of them emphasize sequence modeling, scan design, channel mixing, or transfer rather than explicit handling of nonstationarity.
The present manuscript is positioned at that intersection.
It asks whether a Mamba forecaster benefits when nonstationary structure is made explicit through adaptive normalization, decomposition, and shift-aware fusion, and it evaluates that question against strong non-Mamba baselines under both shared and source-optimized protocols.
