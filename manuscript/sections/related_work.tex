\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Long-Horizon Forecasting}

Neural forecasting architectures encode temporal structure in substantially different ways.
Informer and Reformer reduce the computational cost of attention \cite{informer,reformer}; Autoformer and FEDformer incorporate decomposition \cite{autoformer,fedformer}; and DLinear shows that a small decomposition-based linear model can remain competitive \cite{dlinear}.
PatchTST uses temporal patches, TimesNet maps temporal variation into two-dimensional representations, and iTransformer represents variables rather than time points as tokens \cite{patchtst,timesnet,itransformer}.
N-BEATS and N-HiTS provide further examples of basis expansion and explicit multi-rate structure without attention \cite{nbeats,nhits}.
These alternatives motivate a diverse comparison rather than one restricted to Transformer variants.

\subsection{Nonstationarity and Distribution Shift}

Forecasting under distribution shift has motivated both normalization and architecture-level responses.
RevIN normalizes each input instance and reverses the transformation at the output \cite{revin}.
Non-stationary Transformers argue that normalization can remove predictive information and introduce a learned de-stationary mechanism \cite{nonstationary-transformer}.
Decomposition methods separate trend-like and residual structure so that one representation need not explain all temporal scales \cite{autoformer,fedformer,dlinear}.
The variants evaluated here combine reversible window normalization with decomposition, multiple downsampling rates, or temporal patches, but their component labels denote architectural constructions rather than uniquely identified financial processes.

\subsection{Mamba for Time Series}

Mamba replaces self-attention with an input-selective state-space recurrence \cite{mamba}.
S-Mamba applies bidirectional Mamba blocks over variable tokens for forecasting \cite{smamba}; Bi-Mamba+ studies bidirectionality and token relations \cite{bimamba}; MambaTS examines temporal and variable scan strategies \cite{mambats}; and TimeMachine combines multiple Mamba pathways under channel-mixing and channel-independent settings \cite{timemachine}.
TSMamba further explores transfer through a bidirectional time-series foundation model \cite{tsmamba}.

Several later studies overlap directly with the decomposition and multi-scale ideas considered in this repository.
ms-Mamba uses Mamba blocks operating at multiple temporal sampling rates \cite{msmamba}; KARMA combines multilevel decomposition with multi-scale Mamba blocks for multivariate forecasting \cite{karma}; and DMamba assigns different forecasting modules to decomposed components \cite{dmamba}.
The present study does not claim priority for decomposition or multi-scale Mamba as general principles.
Its narrower purpose is to compare four implemented configurations under a prospectively frozen financial protocol.

\subsection{Financial Forecast Evaluation}

Financial time-series studies are sensitive to the definition of the target, temporal split, regime coverage, and reference forecast \cite{finance-dl-survey}.
For persistent levels, a last-value forecast is an essential benchmark; drift and classical univariate or multivariate models provide additional checks that apparent neural gains are not consequences of an undemanding comparator.
Forecasting competitions likewise show that rankings vary by series and horizon and that simple methods remain competitive \cite{m4,m5}.

The present benchmark therefore reports each task and horizon rather than pooling raw errors across targets with different units.
It uses a fixed future period, repeated neural training, and paired loss comparisons over common forecast origins.
Overlapping origins are handled with moving-block resampling rather than treated as independent observations.
These choices estimate performance within the frozen panels and dates; they do not identify a stable market mechanism or establish economic value.
